EUROCONTROL open performance data

Why Schiphol waits

Arrival flow-management delay at Amsterdam Schiphol, against nine comparable European hubs. 1,033,177 daily airport records, January 2019 to July 2026.

Gilroy Joseph · September 2026
EUROCONTROL figures as downloaded on 16 September 2026.

What I found

Schiphol carries 3.03 minutes of arrival flow-management delay per landing. The median of nine comparable European hubs is 0.73.

Weather is the largest single part of it, and Schiphol has the worst weather delay of the ten. But weather is not what separates it.

Everything after this is how I got there, and the two places where my own argument gives way.

01

Where this comes from

EUROCONTROL's Performance Review Unit publishes one row per airport per day: arrivals flown, delay minutes assigned, and those minutes split across sixteen official cause codes. It runs from January 2019 to 31 July 2026.

Two things this number is not

Not total delayFlow-management delay is assigned before departure, because the destination cannot accept the flight on time. Crew, baggage, boarding and turnaround delays are not in this dataset at all.
Not the airline's numberThis is measured at the airport, so it covers every carrier landing there, not KLM alone.

January to July only, every year. The 2026 data stops on 31 July, so comparing it against full calendar years would have flattered it. Same seven months throughout.

Integrity check before anything else: the sixteen cause columns sum to the reported total exactly, 7,409,233 minutes against 7,409,233 for 2025. No residue, no unallocated remainder.

02

Why these nine

Large Western European hubs built around a dominant network carrier: Heathrow, Paris CDG, Frankfurt, Munich, Zürich, Madrid, Copenhagen, Brussels, Rome Fiumicino.

Istanbul is the only European airport handling more arrivals than Schiphol, and it is excluded: it sits outside the EU regulatory regime, so its capacity constraints are set differently. Barcelona and Gatwick are excluded as point-to-point operations rather than connecting hubs.

Schiphol took 137,318 IFR arrivals in the window. Paris CDG took 137,876 and Heathrow 136,457, so the three largest sit within one percent of each other.

A ranking is not a diagnosis. But it is where you have to start.
03

The ranking

On the second measure Schiphol is second, not first. 4.9% of its arrivals took more than fifteen minutes of flow-management delay, behind Zürich at 5.9%, and ahead of Heathrow and Copenhagen at 2.3%, Paris at 1.8% and Frankfurt at 1.5%.

The obvious answer is weather. A coastal airport, crosswinds, six runways.
04

Where my argument gives way

Weather is the largest single cause at Schiphol, at 1.70 minutes per landing, and Schiphol is the worst of the ten on it. That is real and I am not going to argue it away.

And if you take weather out entirely, Schiphol is not the worst. Zürich is, at 1.735 non-weather minutes per landing against Schiphol's 1.326.

So the simple version of this story does not survive. Here is the version that does.

Zürich's non-weather delay is 1.248 minutes in the code marked Other, an unclassified bucket. Schiphol's sits in a code with a name.

05

The named cause

Aerodrome Capacity is 11.5 times the peer median. It is the airport's own declared ability to accept arrivals, and it is the one line where Schiphol stands apart.

Air traffic control is not the problem. On ATC Capacity and ATC Staffing, Schiphol is better than the peer median, and on staffing it recorded zero.

One year proves nothing. Eight might.
06

Eight years

It predates the pandemic, it survived the pandemic, and it came back larger than it started. This is not a recent operational wobble.

07

Not a handful of bad days

The ten worst days account for 31% of Schiphol's delay minutes. At Munich it is 70%, at Madrid 59%, at Rome 100%.

A high concentration means a few storms. A low one means most days contribute. Schiphol sits at the low end of this set, alongside Zürich at 21% and Heathrow at 31%.

That is the difference between an airport that gets hit occasionally and one that is running close to its ceiling most of the time.

08

What it costs

EUROCONTROL publishes €100 per minute as the network average cost of an ATFM delay minute, and states plainly that it is an indicative figure for general insight, not a number for specific analysis or operational planning.

So treat the euro column as an order of magnitude, not an invoice. It says tens of millions rather than hundreds of thousands, and that is the only claim I am making with it.

Source: EUROCONTROL Standard Inputs for Economic Analyses, release 10.0.2, August 2026, section 16.1, itself drawn from the University of Westminster European airline delay cost reference values, version 4.1.

09

What I would want to know

1

How many hours a week is the declared arrival rate set below the arrivals already scheduled for those hours?

2

When the rate is cut, how long before the schedule is adjusted, and who tells the airlines?

What I would do first

Put two numbers on the daily operations report: minutes of arrival flow-management delay per landing this week, and the same week last year, each split into weather and non-weather.

It needs no new system. It separates the part nobody controls from the part somebody does, which is the argument this whole analysis had to work to reach.

A plan would need their data. This is public data read from the outside.
